Q.01 What is the need of Design Thinking in Engineering? 2 marks

Engineers traditionally focus on technical correctness, but complex real-world problems require solutions centred on human needs. Design Thinking bridges this gap by providing a structured, empathy-driven process for innovation.

  • Human-centred solutions: Ensures products are built for actual user needs, not assumed ones.
  • Handles ambiguity: Provides a framework to define ill-structured problems before solving them.
  • Iterative refinement: Prototyping and testing cycles reduce costly late-stage failures.
  • Cross-disciplinary collaboration: Enables teams from different fields to converge on a shared problem definition.
Q.02 List any two features of Design Thinking. 2 marks
  • Empathy-driven: Design Thinking begins by deeply understanding the user's perspective, emotions, and context — not by assuming requirements. This ensures solutions address real, felt needs.
  • Iterative and non-linear: The process cycles through stages (Empathise → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test) multiple times. Insights from testing feed back into earlier stages, continuously improving the solution.
Q.03 List the steps of the design process. 2 marks

The standard Engineering Design Thinking process consists of seven stages:

  1. Ask — Define the problem, understand context and constraints.
  2. Research / Empathise — Observe and engage with users to gather insights.
  3. Imagine / Ideate — Generate a wide range of possible solutions without judgement.
  4. Plan — Select the best idea and develop a detailed plan.
  5. Prototype — Build a low-fidelity model to make the idea tangible.
  6. Test — Evaluate the prototype with real users and gather feedback.
  7. Improve / Iterate — Refine based on test results and repeat as needed.

Q.05 Discuss the principles of Design Thinking and their role in solving complex problems. 2 marks
  • Human-centredness: Every decision is filtered through the lens of user needs, ensuring the solution is desirable and adopted.
  • Collaboration: Diverse perspectives (engineering, business, arts) are combined — complex problems rarely have single-discipline solutions.
  • Experimentation: Rapid prototyping and testing allow teams to learn cheaply from failure before full-scale investment.
  • Optimism: Believing that every constraint is solvable encourages ambitious thinking rather than incremental fixes.
  • Embrace of ambiguity: Complex problems are ill-defined; DT treats uncertainty as a creative opportunity rather than a blocker.

Together these principles allow teams to reframe poorly-understood problems, explore the solution space broadly, and converge on validated, implementable innovations.

Q.06 Discuss the empathy process and its importance in understanding user needs. 2 marks

Empathy in Design Thinking means putting yourself in the user's position to understand their feelings, frustrations, and aspirations — not just their stated requirements. It is the first and most critical stage.

Methods:

  • Observe: Watch users interact with existing solutions in their natural environment without intervening.
  • Engage: Conduct open-ended interviews to surface latent needs users cannot articulate.
  • Immerse: Experience the user's situation firsthand (e.g., using a wheelchair to design accessibility features).

Importance: Without empathy, engineers solve the wrong problem. Empathy uncovers the gap between what users say they want and what they actually need — the foundation for truly impactful design.

Q.07 Discuss the importance of Design Thinking in engineering with examples. 2 marks

Design Thinking transforms engineering from a purely technical discipline into one that produces human-relevant solutions.

  • Healthcare: IDEO redesigned hospital patient experience by observing patients' anxiety during procedures — leading to redesigned MRI machines that feel less intimidating, reducing sedation rates in children by 80%.
  • Sanitation: Engineers applying DT in rural India discovered women would not use community toilets lacking doors with inside locks — a need invisible to technical designers. Adding simple locks dramatically improved sanitation outcomes.
  • Software: Google's Material Design emerged from DT principles — observing how users instinctively interact with physical objects to create intuitive digital interfaces.

In each case, DT prevented engineers from building technically correct but humanly irrelevant solutions.

Q.08 Explain the role of creative thinking and innovation in Design Thinking. 2 marks

Creative thinking is the fuel of the Ideation stage — it enables teams to move beyond obvious, incremental improvements and generate novel solution concepts.

  • Divergent thinking: Generating the widest possible range of ideas, including wild and impractical ones, to avoid premature convergence on mediocre solutions.
  • Lateral thinking: Approaching problems from unexpected angles — borrowing ideas from unrelated domains (e.g., applying packaging design principles to surgery prep trays).
  • Innovation role: DT provides the structured conditions for innovation — psychological safety to propose impractical ideas, rapid prototyping to test them cheaply, and user feedback to validate them before scale.

Creative thinking without DT's structure produces ideas with no validation. DT without creative thinking produces incremental, predictable outcomes. Together they enable breakthrough innovations.

Q.09 Define brainstorming as an ideation technique. 2 marks

Brainstorming is a group ideation technique in which participants generate a large number of ideas in a short time without criticism or judgement. Introduced by Alex Osborn (1953), it operates on the principle that quantity of ideas precedes quality.

Four rules of classical brainstorming:

  • Defer judgement — no idea is criticised during generation.
  • Encourage wild ideas — unusual ideas often lead to breakthroughs.
  • Build on others' ideas — combine and extend suggestions ("Yes, and…").
  • Go for quantity — the more ideas, the more options to evaluate.
Q.10 Define / Describe Systematic Inventive Thinking (SIT). 2 marks

Systematic Inventive Thinking (SIT) is a structured creativity method developed by Roni Horowitz and Jacob Goldenberg that identifies patterns common to successful innovations and applies them deliberately to generate new product or service ideas from an existing system.

Five SIT operators (patterns):

  • Subtraction — remove an essential component and ask what value can emerge (e.g., cordless drill).
  • Task Unification — assign an additional task to an existing component (e.g., shampoo with conditioner).
  • Multiplication — add a modified copy of an existing component (e.g., razor with multiple blades).
  • Division — separate a component spatially or temporally and reconfigure (e.g., split air conditioner).
  • Attribute Dependency — create or remove dependency between two attributes (e.g., photochromic lenses that darken in sunlight).
Q.11 What is linear innovation? / Why is linear innovation important? 2 marks

Linear innovation refers to incremental, step-by-step improvement of an existing product, process, or service along a predictable, continuous trajectory — building steadily on what already exists without radical disruption.

Importance:

  • Reliability: Organisations can plan investments and roadmaps with predictable outcomes.
  • Optimisation: Cumulative small improvements compound into significant gains over time (e.g., annual smartphone processor improvements).
  • Risk management: Lower risk than disruptive innovation — each step is validated before the next.
  • Foundation: Linear innovation maintains product competitiveness while radical (Box 3) innovation is being developed in parallel.
Q.12 What is meant by latent needs in problem articulation? 2 marks

Latent needs are unexpressed, often unconscious user requirements that users cannot articulate when asked directly — either because they have normalised a frustration, lack the vocabulary to describe it, or cannot envision a better alternative.

Henry Ford's articulation: "If I had asked customers what they wanted, they would have said faster horses." The latent need was faster personal transportation, not literally horses.

How to surface latent needs:

  • Observation in context (ethnographic research)
  • Asking "why" multiple times (5 Whys technique)
  • Empathy mapping — noting what users do vs. what they say

Articulating latent needs correctly is the most valuable output of the Empathise and Define stages.

Q.13 What is meant by Customer Experience (CX)? 2 marks

Customer Experience (CX) is the sum of all interactions, perceptions, and emotions a customer has with a company or product across every touchpoint — from first awareness through purchase, use, and post-purchase support.

  • Cognitive: What customers think about the brand or product.
  • Emotional: How they feel during each interaction.
  • Behavioural: How they act in response (loyalty, advocacy, complaints).

CX is distinct from customer service (a single touchpoint) — it encompasses the entire journey. Companies that design CX intentionally (e.g., Apple Stores, Swiggy's delivery tracking) achieve higher loyalty, NPS scores, and revenue.

Q.14 What is meant by an Affinity Diagram? 2 marks

An Affinity Diagram (KJ Method, developed by Jiro Kawakita) is a bottom-up synthesis tool that organises a large volume of qualitative data — observations, ideas, user quotes, research notes — into natural thematic clusters to reveal patterns.

Process:

  1. Write each data point or observation on a separate sticky note.
  2. Silently group related notes together without debate.
  3. Create header cards that capture the theme of each cluster.
  4. Identify relationships between clusters to form a higher-level picture.

Use: After user research interviews, an affinity diagram transforms hundreds of raw observations into 5–8 actionable insight themes that can drive problem definition.

Q.16 Define customer journey map. 2 marks

A Customer Journey Map is a visual narrative that tracks a customer's end-to-end experience with a product or service — from first awareness through to post-purchase — highlighting their actions, thoughts, emotions, and pain points at each stage.

Key components:

  • Stages / Phases — the sequential steps (Awareness → Consideration → Purchase → Use → Loyalty)
  • Touchpoints — every interaction point between the customer and the organisation
  • Actions — what the customer does at each stage
  • Thoughts & Emotions — the customer's mental state (frustration, delight, confusion)
  • Pain Points & Opportunities — where experience breaks down and where improvement is possible
Q.17 What is the service experience cycle? 2 marks

The Service Experience Cycle describes the complete, closed loop of a customer's interaction with a service — from initial contact to completion and follow-up — including every touchpoint that shapes the overall perception.

Stages:

  1. Pre-service — Awareness, search, booking/reservation
  2. Arrival — First physical or digital contact, reception
  3. Core service delivery — The main service interaction
  4. Supplementary services — Support elements (billing, assistance, amenities)
  5. Departure — Checkout, completion
  6. Post-service — Follow-up, feedback, loyalty programmes

Example: A hospital patient cycle includes appointment booking → reception → consultation → treatment → discharge → follow-up call. A breakdown at any stage damages the overall experience.

Q.18 Define sustainable design. 2 marks

Sustainable design (also called eco-design or green design) is the practice of designing products, systems, and services that meet present human needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own — minimising environmental impact across the entire product lifecycle.

Three pillars (Triple Bottom Line):

  • Environmental: Minimise resource consumption, pollution, waste, and carbon footprint.
  • Social: Ensure safe, equitable, and inclusive outcomes for users and communities.
  • Economic: Maintain viability and long-term value — sustainability must be cost-effective to be adopted at scale.
Q.19 What is recycling in design? / State importance of recycling in design. 2 marks

Recycling in design refers to intentionally designing products so that materials can be recovered and reprocessed at end-of-life — either to make new products of the same type (closed-loop) or new products of lower specification (open-loop).

Importance:

  • Resource conservation: Reduces demand for virgin raw materials (metals, polymers, glass).
  • Energy savings: Recycled aluminium requires only 5% of the energy needed to produce primary aluminium.
  • Landfill reduction: Extends the useful life of materials, reducing waste and associated pollution.
  • Design for disassembly: Encourages modular design with fewer material types, making products easier to repair and upgrade.
Q.20 What is eco-friendly design? 2 marks

Eco-friendly design is the intentional creation of products, services, and systems that have a minimal negative impact on the natural environment throughout their entire lifecycle — from raw material extraction through manufacturing, use, and disposal.

Key principles:

  • Use of renewable, biodegradable, or recycled materials
  • Energy-efficient manufacturing and operation
  • Design for longevity and repairability (vs. planned obsolescence)
  • Elimination or reduction of toxic substances
  • Minimal packaging using compostable materials

Example: Fairphone designs modular smartphones with replaceable components — users can swap a broken screen or battery without professional help, dramatically extending device life.

Q.21 What is Design for Environment (DFE)? 2 marks

Design for Environment (DFE) is a systematic engineering approach that integrates environmental considerations into the product design and development process from the earliest stages — rather than addressing environmental issues as an afterthought during manufacturing or disposal.

Core DFE strategies:

  • Design for disassembly: Components can be easily separated for repair or recycling.
  • Material selection: Preference for recyclable, non-toxic, low-impact materials.
  • Life Cycle Assessment (LCA): Quantifying environmental impact across all lifecycle stages.
  • Energy minimisation: Reduce energy consumption during manufacture and use.
  • Take-back programmes: Design products for manufacturer-managed end-of-life recovery.
Q.22 What is prototyping in design thinking? 2 marks

Prototyping in Design Thinking is the process of creating quick, inexpensive, tangible representations of ideas to test hypotheses and gather user feedback before full-scale development. It makes abstract concepts concrete and testable.

Fidelity spectrum:

  • Low-fidelity: Paper sketches, cardboard models, role-play scripts — used early to test core concepts cheaply.
  • Medium-fidelity: Foam models, wireframes, clickable mockups — test specific interactions.
  • High-fidelity: Functional prototypes close to the final product — validate technical feasibility and user experience.

Principle: "Prototype to think, not to prove." The goal is learning, not perfection. A prototype that fails teaches more than a hypothesis that was never tested.

Q.23 List challenges in diverse team collaboration. 2 marks
  • Communication barriers: Different disciplines use domain-specific jargon that creates misunderstandings between, say, engineers and marketing professionals.
  • Conflicting priorities: Team members from different functions may have different success metrics (technical robustness vs. business viability vs. user desirability).
  • Cultural and cognitive differences: Diverse backgrounds bring varied problem-solving approaches — valuable but requiring active facilitation to prevent friction.
  • Power dynamics: Dominant voices from senior members or technical experts can suppress creative contributions from others.
  • Coordination overhead: More perspectives mean more alignment meetings and decision complexity, slowing iteration cycles.

Q.01Describe the seven steps of the Engineering Design Thinking process in detail.10 marks

The Engineering Design Thinking process is a human-centred, iterative framework that guides teams from problem discovery to validated solutions. Unlike linear engineering design, it embraces ambiguity, iteration, and empathy at every stage.

7-Step Engineering Design Thinking Process
Step 1 — Ask (Problem Definition)

The Ask phase transforms a broad, vague challenge into a focused, actionable problem statement. Teams ask: What is the actual problem? Who is affected? What are the constraints? Techniques include stakeholder interviews, scope mapping, and constraint analysis. A well-framed problem statement prevents the team from solving the wrong problem — the most expensive mistake in engineering.

Step 2 — Research / Empathise

Teams immerse themselves in the user's world through observation, contextual interviews, shadowing, and immersive experience. The goal is to uncover latent needs — the unstated frustrations and aspirations users cannot articulate. Outputs include interview notes, empathy maps, personas, and user journey maps that capture the full human context.

Step 3 — Imagine / Ideate

With a deep understanding of user needs, teams generate the widest possible range of solution concepts using techniques such as brainstorming, SCAMPER, SIT (Systematic Inventive Thinking), mind mapping, and bodystorming. Judgement is deferred — quantity precedes quality. A team should aim for 50–100 ideas before evaluating any.

Step 4 — Plan

The most promising ideas (selected through dot-voting, impact-feasibility matrices, or user feedback) are developed into a detailed implementation plan. This includes defining materials, components, timelines, and resource requirements. The plan bridges ideation and physical creation — it answers "how exactly will we build this?"

Step 5 — Prototype

A tangible, low-cost representation of the solution is built rapidly. Prototypes can be physical (cardboard, foam, 3D print), digital (wireframe, interactive mockup), or experiential (service blueprint enacted by team members). The prototype's only purpose is to test hypotheses and provoke feedback — it does not need to be functional or beautiful.

Step 6 — Test

The prototype is placed in the hands of real target users in realistic conditions. Teams observe — not guide — how users interact with it. Key questions: Does it solve the user's problem? What causes confusion? What delights them unexpectedly? What breaks? Testing generates the highest-quality insights of any DT stage.

Step 7 — Improve / Iterate

Findings from testing feed back into earlier stages. Minor issues → refine the prototype. Major misalignments → return to Ideate or even Empathise. This iterative cycle continues until the solution reliably solves the user's problem. In practice, multiple rounds of prototype-test-improve are completed before development begins.

Q.02Describe the 3-Box framework and Box-3 Ideation, highlighting role in future-oriented innovation.10 marks

The 3-Box Framework, developed by Vijay Govindarajan (Dartmouth), is a strategic innovation model that explains why successful companies struggle with radical innovation and provides a structure for managing it alongside existing operations simultaneously.

The Three Boxes
BoxNameFocusTime HorizonExample
Box 1Manage the PresentOptimise and maximise the value of the current business model through linear innovationToday (0–2 years)Improving fuel efficiency of an existing car engine
Box 2Selectively Forget the PastIdentify and abandon capabilities, values, and beliefs that block future growth — even profitable onesTransition (1–3 years)A newspaper abandoning print-first thinking to embrace digital
Box 3Create the FutureDevelop breakthrough innovations and new business models by experimenting with weak signals of future trendsFuture (3–10 years)Toyota developing hydrogen fuel cell vehicles while still selling petrol cars
The Core Tension

Box 1 activities are performance-driven — they reward predictability, efficiency, and risk avoidance. Box 3 activities are exploration-driven — they require tolerance for ambiguity, experimentation, and failure. Most companies unconsciously allocate 90%+ of resources to Box 1, starving future innovation. The framework explicitly allocates separate leadership, budgets, and metrics for each box.

Box-3 Ideation Process
  1. Scan for weak signals: Identify emerging trends, technologies, or social changes at the periphery of the current business that could reshape the industry in 5–10 years.
  2. Challenge Box 1 assumptions: Ask "What if our core product became irrelevant? What value would we still provide?"
  3. Hypothesis experiments: Run low-cost experiments (probes) to test assumptions about future states — not to build a product, but to learn.
  4. Forget selectively (Box 2): Identify which current capabilities and mental models must be deliberately abandoned to allow Box 3 to succeed.
  5. Incubate separately: Protect Box 3 projects from Box 1 performance metrics — evaluate them on learning milestones, not revenue.
3-Box Model — Simultaneous Innovation Management
Role in Future-Oriented Innovation

The 3-Box framework addresses the innovator's dilemma — the phenomenon where market-leading companies are disrupted by innovations they could have invented themselves, but didn't because short-term financial incentives prevented investment. By institutionalising Box 3 as a permanent, separately-resourced function, companies like GE (Ecomagination), Amazon (AWS originated as Box 3), and Reliance (Jio was Box 3 for a petroleum conglomerate) created entirely new revenue streams that eventually exceeded their original businesses.

Q.03Explain the SIT methods (Addition, Subtraction, Multiplication, Division, Task Unification) with examples.10 marks

Systematic Inventive Thinking (SIT) is built on the insight that most successful innovations share a small set of structural patterns, called thinking tools. By deliberately applying these patterns to an existing product or service — working within the closed world of its components — designers can generate novel, implementable ideas systematically rather than randomly.

SIT ToolOperationProduct ExampleService Example
SubtractionRemove a component that seems essential and imagine what new benefit or product resultsCordless drill (remove the cord) → greater portability; Seedless watermelon (remove seeds)Self-checkout (remove cashier) → faster throughput; Vending machine (remove salesperson)
Task UnificationAssign an additional task to an existing internal or external componentShampoo + conditioner in one bottle; Tyre rim designed to also seal a puncture (run-flat tyre)ATM that also accepts deposits; Nurse who also schedules follow-up appointments during consultation
MultiplicationCopy a component, then modify the copy — not just add a duplicateMulti-blade razor (multiple blades at different angles progressively lift the hair); Bifocal lenses (modified copy of the lens for near/far vision)Express lane + standard lane in supermarkets (modified copy of the checkout experience)
DivisionDivide a component into parts, then rearrange spatially or temporallySplit air conditioner (compressor outside, blower inside — separated spatially); Timed-release capsule medication (temporally divided dosage)Hospital triage (divide patient flow by urgency); Pre-check-in online (temporally divide hotel check-in)

The Closed-World Principle

A key insight of SIT is the Closed World principle: the best innovations are typically found by working within the existing components and environment of the product — not by adding entirely new technologies or resources from outside. This constraint forces creativity and ensures feasibility. When engineers say "we need to add a new sensor," SIT asks: "Which existing component could perform that sensing function if reassigned?"

Worked Example — Smart Classroom Design Using SIT
  • Subtraction: Remove the physical textbook → students access content through tablets; reduces weight and enables dynamic updates.
  • Task Unification: Assign the classroom desk the additional task of charging devices wirelessly — the surface becomes a Qi charging pad.
  • Division: Divide the lecture temporally — pre-recorded content at home (flipped classroom), classroom time used only for discussion and problem-solving.
  • Attribute Dependency: Create dependency between student attention (detected via eye-tracking) and content delivery speed — the system slows down when attention drops.

Q.05Explain the concepts of weak signals and future innovation strategies.10 marks

A weak signal (term from Ansoff, 1975) is an early, ambiguous, fragmented indicator of an emerging trend, technology, or social change that has not yet become mainstream — but which, if correctly interpreted, could represent a major future opportunity or threat. Weak signals are the raw material of Box 3 / non-linear innovation.

Characteristics of Weak Signals
PropertyWeak SignalStrong Signal
ClarityAmbiguous, incomplete, contradictoryClear, confirmed, widely reported
Timing5–15 years before mainstream impactCurrently visible in the market
SourceFringe researchers, startups, marginal usersMajor companies, mainstream media
Response windowWide — time to experiment and positionNarrow — competitors already responding
Risk of misinterpretationHigh — most weak signals never materialiseLow — impact is evident
Historical Examples of Weak Signals
  • Internet (1989): The WWW was a weak signal visible only to academics — companies like Amazon and Google that read it correctly built trillion-dollar businesses. Retailers who dismissed it were disrupted.
  • Mobile payments (2005–2008): M-Pesa in Kenya was a weak signal about mobile money transfer — Indian banks that read it early developed UPI infrastructure that now processes 10 billion transactions/month.
  • Plant-based protein (2010): A fringe food movement was a weak signal — companies like Beyond Meat that acted early now supply McDonald's and KFC globally.
Future Innovation Strategies Based on Weak Signals
  1. Environmental scanning: Systematically monitor fringe publications, startup activity, academic research, and social media at the periphery of your industry to detect signals early.
  2. Scenario planning: Develop 3–4 plausible future scenarios based on detected weak signals. For each scenario, ask: "What capabilities would we need? What threats would we face?"
  3. Small experiments (probes): Rather than betting the company on a weak signal, run small, cheap experiments to test whether it is amplifying. If it is, scale; if not, abandon with minimal loss.
  4. Platform building: Develop modular capabilities that can be quickly reconfigured as signals clarify — rather than rigid, signal-specific products.
  5. Ecosystem monitoring: Track which startups are attracting venture capital in adjacent spaces — VC funding patterns often amplify weak signals 5–7 years before mainstream adoption.

The strategic imperative: companies should allocate 10–15% of innovation investment to weak signal exploration. The 90% that don't materialise are the cost of not missing the 10% that transform industries.

Q.06Describe the brainstorming process and its role in generating innovative solutions.10 marks

Brainstorming, introduced by advertising executive Alex Osborn in 1953, is the most widely used ideation technique in Design Thinking. It is a structured group creativity process designed to generate the maximum quantity and diversity of ideas within a short time frame by suspending critical judgement.

Complete Brainstorming Process
  1. Preparation (before the session): Define and clearly display the problem statement ("How Might We…" format). Assemble a diverse team (5–8 people from different disciplines). Prepare materials: sticky notes, markers, large wall space or whiteboard. Set a time limit (30–45 minutes for idea generation).
  2. Warm-up (5 minutes): Run a quick creative warm-up exercise (e.g., "list 30 uses for a brick in 2 minutes") to switch participants from analytical to generative thinking mode and create psychological safety.
  3. Idea generation (20–30 minutes): Each participant writes one idea per sticky note and places it on the wall or announces it to the group. The facilitator enforces the four rules (no judgement, go for quantity, wild ideas welcome, build on others' ideas). The facilitator also re-stimulates with provocations: "What if cost was not a constraint?" "What would a child do?" "What is the opposite of the problem?"
  4. Clustering (10 minutes): Group similar or related ideas into themes — this is where an Affinity Diagram is created from the raw output.
  5. Selection (10 minutes): Use dot-voting (each participant gets 3–5 dots) to identify the most promising ideas for further development. The top ideas are shortlisted for prototyping.

Role in Generating Innovative Solutions

Brainstorming's power lies in three mechanisms: cognitive diversity (different backgrounds produce non-overlapping idea spaces), idea mutation (one person's half-formed idea triggers a complete idea in another's mind), and psychological safety (the no-judgement rule allows impractical ideas — which are often the seeds of breakthrough solutions — to survive long enough to be refined). Research by IDEO confirms that the best solutions in design projects almost never come from the first round of brainstorming; they emerge from combining and mutating ideas across multiple sessions.

  • Creates shared understanding: Replaces individual assumptions about the user with a collectively validated picture — all team members work from the same mental model.
  • Reveals contradictions: The gap between what users Say/Do vs. Think/Feel is the richest source of latent need insights. Rekha says she follows the diet but doesn't — why? That "why" is the design opportunity.
  • Drives problem definition: The empathy map's pains and gains feed directly into the Point-of-View (POV) statement: "[User] needs a [need] because [insight]."

Service Design is the application of Design Thinking principles specifically to the design of service experiences — ensuring that every touchpoint is useful, usable, efficient, and delightful from the customer's perspective and deliverable, effective, and distinctive from the organisation's perspective.

Service Design Process — Six Stages
Service Design and Development Process
Stage-by-Stage Detail

Stage 1 — Discovery and Research: Teams conduct contextual user research — shadowing customers through their service journey, interviewing frontline staff, and mapping current pain points. Service Safari (experiencing competitor services as a customer) is a key tool here.

Q.09Explain the concept of a mind map and its role in idea generation.10 marks

Structure of a Mind Map
Mind Map Structure — Smart Healthcare App
How to Create a Mind Map
  1. Write the central problem or concept in the middle of a blank page.
  2. Draw thick branches radiating outward for major themes or categories.
  3. Add thinner sub-branches for specific ideas, associations, or sub-categories.
  4. Use colours, images, and keywords (not sentences) — the brain responds to visual cues.
  5. Let associations flow freely — connect branches if two ideas are related.
Role in Idea Generation

Overcoming linear thinking: Unlike lists or outlines, mind maps externalise the brain's associative structure. Ideas that are spatially near often combine unexpectedly — the branch "Reminders" next to "Community" might spark the idea of peer accountability reminders for medication adherence.

Divergent expansion: Mind maps encourage "yes, and" thinking — each node can be expanded indefinitely without disrupting the whole structure. This prevents premature closure on the first good idea.

Team convergence: A collaborative mind map created on a whiteboard during a DT session makes everyone's associations visible to the whole team, triggering cross-pollination of ideas across disciplinary boundaries.

Problem reframing: Sometimes the most valuable outcome of a mind map is discovering that the problem was framed too narrowly — branches that feel distant from the centre often reveal the actual root cause or a broader opportunity.

Q.12Discuss how combining ideas leads to complex innovation concepts with examples.10 marks

One of the most powerful and underappreciated principles of Design Thinking ideation is that the most innovative solutions rarely come from a single idea — they emerge from the combination, collision, and fusion of multiple partial ideas. This is sometimes called "combinatorial innovation."

Why Combination Drives Innovation

The history of technology is largely the history of ideas recombined: the iPhone combined an iPod + phone + internet communicator. Uber combined GPS + smartphones + excess car capacity + payments. The printing press combined a screw press (used for wine) with movable type. None of these required invention of a new fundamental technology — they required the creative recombination of existing concepts.

Mechanisms of Idea Combination in DT
MechanismHow It WorksExample
Direct combinationTwo separate product/service ideas are merged into one that serves both needs simultaneouslyCafé + co-working space + bookstore (Third Wave Coffee) — combines three separate concepts into one experience
Cross-domain borrowingA solution from one industry is applied to solve a problem in a completely different domainHospital adopted pit-stop crew coordination from Formula 1 racing → redesigned ICU procedures to reduce patient handover errors by 40%
Analogical transferThe structure of a solution to a known problem is transferred to solve an analogous, unrelated problemWikipedia's collaborative editing model (from open-source software development) applied to encyclopaedia content creation
Feature fusion (SIT Task Unification)A component of one product is given the function of another component from a different productCamera added to phone → smartphone; Microphone added to headphone → hands-free calling headset
Worked DT Example — Smart Rural Health Kiosk

Suppose a DT team is working on healthcare access in rural areas. Their brainstorming produces these separate ideas: (A) Telemedicine video calls to city doctors; (B) ATM-style self-service machines for government services; (C) Community health workers who visit villages; (D) WhatsApp-based appointment booking; (E) Point-of-care diagnostic devices (glucometer, BP monitor).

Individually, none of these ideas solves the problem completely. But combining A + B + D + E yields: a solar-powered village health kiosk (B) with a touchscreen for WhatsApp booking (D), video consultation screen (A), and attached diagnostic devices (E) operated by the community health worker (C) — a complex, integrated solution that none of the individual ideas could achieve.

Techniques for Facilitating Idea Combination
  • Forced association: Randomly pair two unrelated ideas and ask "How could these work together?" The constraint forces creative connection.
  • Morphological matrix: List key parameters of the design across rows and different options for each parameter across columns. Select one option per row in non-obvious combinations.
  • Concept mash-up cards: Write each idea on a card and physically shuffle and pair them — teams explore every combination systematically.
  • "Yes, and" building: In a group, each participant adds to the previous person's idea without negating it — ideas accrete into complex concepts organically.

The implication for DT practice: teams should resist selecting the "best single idea" too early. The optimal strategy is to generate 50–100 ideas, then invest in exploring 10–15 promising combinations before converging on a solution concept.

Q.14Describe the five stages of the product life cycle in detail.10 marks

The Product Life Cycle (PLC) model describes the sequential stages a product passes through from its introduction to market through its eventual decline and withdrawal — each stage characterised by different levels of sales, competition, profitability, and strategic priorities.

Product Life Cycle — Sales & Profit Curve
Stage 1 — Introduction

The product enters the market. Sales are low because awareness is limited and customers are uncertain about the new offering. Costs are high — R&D costs are being amortised, marketing spend is heavy to build awareness, and production volumes are low so unit costs are high. Profit is typically negative. The design challenge here is communicating value and ensuring ease of first use to overcome adoption resistance. Example: Electric vehicles in 2010 — low sales, heavy Tesla investment in showrooms and test drive experiences to overcome scepticism.

Stage 2 — Growth

Sales accelerate as word-of-mouth spreads and early adopters endorse the product. Competitors enter the market, attracted by the growing opportunity. Profits increase rapidly as production volume drives unit cost down. Design priority shifts to feature differentiation and scaling the experience to serve a larger, more mainstream audience. Example: Smartphones 2008–2012 — explosive sales growth; multiple competitors entering; constant feature innovation.

Stage 3 — Maturity

Sales growth slows as the market becomes saturated. Competition intensifies — multiple products offer similar features. Profit margins compress as companies cut prices to maintain share. Design focus shifts to cost efficiency, incremental improvements, and user experience refinement rather than breakthrough features. Example: Laptops 2015–2022 — incremental improvements in battery life and weight; market share battles between Dell, HP, Lenovo, and Apple.

Stage 4 — Saturation

The market is fully penetrated — virtually all potential buyers already own the product. Replacement demand is the primary driver of sales. Price competition is intense. Companies attempt market extension through new segments, geographies, or product variations. Design Thinking is critical here for product reinvention — using DT's empathise-ideate cycle to find the next non-linear innovation before decline sets in. Example: Petrol car market in developed countries — manufacturers now pivoting to EVs to avoid decline.

Stage 5 — Decline

Sales fall as newer technologies or changing consumer preferences make the product obsolete. Companies must decide whether to harvest (reduce investment, extract remaining cash), divest (sell the product line), or niche (serve a small loyal segment that still needs the product). DT's role here is in sustainability design — managing end-of-life responsibly through recycling programmes and Design for Environment principles. Example: Film cameras after digital photography; landline telephones; physical encyclopaedias.

Optimisation targetEach subsystem optimised independently; HVAC engineer minimises HVAC cost without knowing the thermal design of the envelopeWhole system optimised together; a better-insulated building envelope (architecture) allows a smaller HVAC system — total cost lower

Application in Building Design (Net-Zero Architecture)

The Bullitt Center in Seattle (net-zero energy, net-zero water) demonstrates integrative design: the building orientation was decided in a combined session by the architect (daylighting), structural engineer (wind loading), mechanical engineer (passive ventilation), and solar engineer (PV optimisation). The roof was designed as a single surface that simultaneously carries structural load, supports solar panels, collects rainwater, and provides a green roof — one element serving four functions. This would be impossible in fragmented design where each specialist designs their own roof component.

Application in Product Design

Tesla's integrative design of the Model S demonstrates the concept at product scale. Rather than adapting an existing car chassis to carry a battery, the battery pack was designed simultaneously with the chassis — becoming a structural element of the floor, lowering the centre of gravity, eliminating the transmission tunnel, and enabling the flat interior floor. The battery simultaneously serves energy storage, structural, and interior design functions — an integrative design outcome that no sequential specialist design process could have produced.

Integrative Design and Design Thinking

Design Thinking naturally enables integrative design through:

  • Cross-disciplinary teams: DT explicitly assembles teams from different fields, creating the conditions for integrated problem-solving.
  • Systems-level problem framing: The "How Might We" question targets the whole user experience, not a single component — preventing the team from prematurely narrowing to one subsystem.
  • Co-creation of prototypes: When engineers, architects, and users build a prototype together, they naturally discover interdependencies that are invisible when each discipline prototypes separately.

Integrative design is not a technique to be applied at a single stage — it is a mindset that must pervade the entire DT process from problem definition to final testing. Its adoption is the defining difference between organisations that produce good products and those that produce exceptional ones.

Problem statement: "How might we reduce vehicle wait time and accident risk at Secunderabad X roads while improving traffic flow for all road users — including pedestrians, cyclists, and emergency vehicles?"

Step 2 — Research / Empathise

Field observation (7 AM–9 AM, 5 PM–8 PM) reveals: average wait time = 4.2 minutes per cycle; emergency vehicles blocked an average 8.3 minutes per incident; pedestrians cross against red lights 67% of the time (because cycles are too long); auto-rickshaw drivers cut signal by entering the box junction. Brainstorming generates 60+ ideas. Selected top concepts after affinity clustering:

  • Adaptive signal control using computer vision to count vehicle queues per lane in real time
  • Emergency vehicle preemption using GPS and siren detection

  • Variable signal timing based on time-of-day and real-time queue length

System architecture defined: (1) 8 CCTV cameras per intersection with edge AI counting vehicles per lane. (2) Central STMS server receiving real-time data, running queue-minimisation algorithm. Q.22Describe the complete DT process for a Smart Irrigation System for farmers.10 marks

A Smart Irrigation System for smallholder farmers in semi-arid Telangana is used to demonstrate the complete DT process applied to a socially impactful engineering problem.

Step 1 — Ask

HMW statement: "How might we help small-scale farmers in Nalgonda district irrigate their crops more efficiently to reduce water waste and improve yield — using technology they can operate and maintain themselves?"

Key constraints: farmers have feature phones (not smartphones), operate on less than Rs 5,000 disposable income/month, wells are borewells 200–300 feet deep, major crops are cotton and paddy.

Step 2 — Research / Empathise

Field visits to 20 farms over three weeks reveal latent needs. Farmers wake at 4 AM to start pumps manually, losing sleep. They irrigate based on schedule ("Monday and Thursday"), not soil moisture — leading to over-irrigation during monsoon and under-irrigation during peak summer. Women bear most of the manual labour of managing irrigation channels. Electricity to run pumps is only available 6 hours/day (night hours) — farmers miss these windows when they oversleep. Mobile signal is weak but SMS works reliably.

Step 3 — Ideate
  • Soil moisture sensors triggering automatic pump start/stop
  • SMS alerts to farmers when soil moisture falls below threshold
  • Scheduled pump control via SMS commands (farmer texts "START" to activate pump)
  • Solar-powered sensors to eliminate dependency on grid power for sensing
  • Shared community-level sensor dashboard maintained by an agri-extension officer
  • IFFCO Kisan integration to deliver crop-specific irrigation advice via SMS
Step 4 — Plan

System design: (1) Capacitive soil moisture sensor buried at crop root depth, solar-powered. (2) GSM module transmits sensor reading to cloud server twice daily. (3) Cloud server sends SMS alert to farmer's feature phone when moisture falls below crop-specific threshold. (4) Farmer replies by SMS to activate borewell pump via GSM-controlled relay. (5) Pump auto-shuts off after calculated duration based on field area and sensor reading. (6) Agricultural extension officer receives weekly summary report.

Step 5 — Prototype

Low-fidelity: A manual demonstration using a chart showing how moisture levels change with irrigation, walked through with farmers in a chaupal session — validates that the concept matches their mental model. Hardware prototype: one farmer's plot equipped with sensor, GSM module, and relay. Run for 4 weeks with farmer receiving daily SMS — compare water use and yield against adjacent un-equipped plot.

Step 6 — Test

Pilot results: 34% reduction in water use (critical — the borewell was running dry); 22% increase in cotton yield (soil not oscillating between over-wet and over-dry). Key failure: farmers did not always reply to SMS within the required window — pump did not activate, crop stressed. Discovery: farmers wanted automatic pump activation, not manual confirmation via SMS — they trusted the system after 2 weeks. Two farmers could not read the SMS — needed audio call alert. Women farmers reported the system eliminated the need to wake at 4 AM.

Step 7 — Improve

Iteration: (1) Added auto-activation mode — farmer confirms once ("I want auto-mode") and system activates pump automatically when moisture threshold is breached. (2) Added IVR (interactive voice response) alert — phone call with pre-recorded message for low-literacy users. (3) Added OTP confirmation for pump start to prevent accidental activation. System scaled to 150 farms in Phase 2 through NABARD grant funding.

Q.23Discuss concept of modular design and prototype development in interdisciplinary projects.10 marks

Modular design is an engineering approach that partitions a complex system into self-contained, interchangeable modules with standardised interfaces — each module performing a specific, well-defined function that can be designed, tested, and improved independently of other modules.

Principles of Modular Design
PrincipleDescriptionBenefit
Functional decompositionSystem is divided into modules each responsible for one clearly bounded function — single responsibility principleSimplifies design, testing, and maintenance; failures are isolated to the module
Standardised interfacesModules connect through defined, standardised physical or logical interfaces — any compatible module can be substitutedEnables parallel development by different teams; modules can be upgraded independently
IndependenceChanging one module's internal design should not require changes to other modules — low coupling, high cohesionReduces coordination overhead; enables specialisation by discipline
ReusabilityModules developed for one product can be reused in another if interfaces are compatibleAmortises development cost; reduces time-to-market for product variants
Modular Design in Interdisciplinary DT Projects

In an interdisciplinary capstone project (e.g., a Smart Healthcare Wearable involving ECE, CS, Mechanical, and Biomedical Engineering students), modular design enables each discipline to own a clearly bounded module:

ModuleResponsible DisciplineInterface to Other Modules
Sensor array (ECG, SpO₂, temperature)ECEStandardised ADC output protocol; I2C/SPI bus interface
Signal processing and ML inferenceCS / AIReceives raw ADC data; outputs classified health event flags
Wearable enclosure and ergonomicsMechanical EngineeringDefines physical dimensions and mounting points that ECE sensor PCB must fit
Clinical validation and alert thresholdsBiomedical EngineeringDefines threshold values used by CS module for alarm generation
Mobile application and data storageCSReceives BLE data from ECE module; stores to cloud; displays to cardiologist
Prototype Development Strategy for Modular Interdisciplinary Projects
  1. Interface specification first: Before any module is built, all teams define and agree on the interfaces between modules. This contract allows parallel development — ECE builds the sensor module while CS builds the processing algorithm, both working to the same data format specification.
  2. Module-level prototypes: Each team builds and tests their module independently — ECE validates sensor accuracy on a bench test rig; CS validates the ML model on pre-recorded datasets; Mechanical validates ergonomics with foam mockups and user fitting sessions.
  3. Integration prototype: Modules are assembled for the first time — integration testing reveals interface incompatibilities (e.g., the CS module expects 250 Hz sampling but ECE module delivers 200 Hz). These are resolved at integration, not during full-system design.
  4. User prototype: The integrated system is worn by real users (healthcare volunteers, not team members) in realistic conditions — sitting, exercising, sleeping. Observations that no module-level test can reveal (the strap chafes after 4 hours; the app crashes when phone locks; the alarm is too quiet in a hospital setting) are captured and fed into the next iteration.

Modular prototyping in DT interdisciplinary projects is not just an engineering convenience — it is the social architecture that makes diverse teams function. When each discipline has a clearly bounded scope, the collaboration becomes productive rather than territorial.

Q.24Explain the stages of a DT capstone project for a Smart Healthcare Monitoring System.10 marks

A Smart Healthcare Monitoring System (SHMS) for post-discharge cardiac patients — enabling remote monitoring from home with automatic escalation to their cardiologist — is used as the capstone project context for all five DT units.

Stage 1 — Empathise (Unit 1)

The interdisciplinary team (ECE, CS, Biomedical, Mechanical, Design students) spends two weeks in Apollo Hospital's cardiology ward — observing patients, shadowing nurses, and interviewing cardiologists. Key insights: 40% of readmissions happen within 30 days of discharge; patients do not monitor themselves at home because existing devices are confusing; families are anxious but don't know when to be alarmed; cardiologists have no visibility into patients between appointments. Empathy maps are created for three personas: post-MI patient (60 M, low tech literacy), family caregiver (35 F, moderate smartphone user), and cardiologist (limited time, needs actionable summaries).

Stage 2 — Define (Unit 1)

Point-of-View statements: "Ramu, 60-year-old post-MI patient, needs a monitoring system that tells him 'you're okay' without requiring him to interpret numbers — because his fear of bad news makes him avoid checking." "Dr. Rao needs a daily patient status summary that alerts him only when action is required — because he sees 50 patients/day and cannot check dashboards between consultations." These POV statements drive all subsequent design decisions.

Stage 3 — Ideate (Unit 2)

Brainstorming generates 75 ideas. Top concepts after dot-voting: (1) A wristband with a simple traffic light display — green/yellow/red — patient never sees numbers. (2) Daily "health score" SMS to patient and family — a single number (0–100) replacing raw ECG data. (3) Automatic escalation: if the health score drops below threshold for 48 hours, system sends alert to cardiologist's pager. (4) Family view: a simple mobile app showing only the traffic light and last 7-day trend. Ideas are combined using SIT Task Unification — the wristband also serves as a fall detector (accelerometer already present).

Stage 4 — Prototype (Unit 5)

Three parallel prototypes:

  • Physical: Mechanical team builds a foam + cardboard wristband mockup in 3 hours. Tested with 8 patients for comfort and strap closure usability — 3 design iterations on strap buckle before reaching acceptable solution.
  • Digital UI: CS team creates Figma clickable prototype of family app. Tested with 5 family caregivers — all could read the traffic light; 3 did not understand the 7-day chart format → simplified to just "Better/Same/Worse" compared to yesterday.
  • Algorithm: Biomedical team validates the health-score algorithm on 100 de-identified patient records from Apollo — tests sensitivity (correctly identifies deteriorating patients) and specificity (does not alarm unnecessarily).
Stage 5 — Test and Iterate (Unit 5)

System pilot with 15 discharged patients over 6 weeks: 2 patients had their cardiologist alerted by the system → both confirmed by cardiologist to be genuine deteriorations that would have otherwise been undetected until symptoms became severe. 3 patients stopped wearing the device after Week 2 → exit interview revealed strap was irritating during sleep → design change: removable night strap.

Sustainability and Environmental Considerations (Unit 4)

Device casing designed from recycled ABS; battery swappable by user (Design for Disassembly); take-back programme for end-of-life device with hospital as collection point; no mercury components (RoHS compliant).

Interdisciplinary Design Thinking teams — combining engineers, designers, clinicians, social scientists, and business professionals — produce the most innovative solutions but also generate the most friction. Understanding and systematically resolving these challenges is a core DT leadership competency.

Structural Practices for High-Functioning Diverse Teams
  • Psychological safety: Amy Edmondson's research (Google Project Aristotle) found psychological safety — the belief that it is safe to take interpersonal risks — is the single strongest predictor of team performance. Establishing "all ideas are valid here" norms from day one is the DT team leader's primary responsibility.
  • Regular retrospectives: Weekly 15-minute team retrospectives ("What's working? What's not? What should we try?") surface process problems before they become team fractures.
  • Physical co-location: When possible, co-locating diverse team members in a shared design studio dramatically accelerates trust and informal communication — reducing the coordination cost of diversity.
